A sign painter must copy a small drawing onto a board at twice the size. Measured point by point, the work takes hours and every measured point adds a small error. A pantograph does it in one pass: you move a stylus along the small drawing and a pen draws the large one, with every length multiplied by the same ratio. The ratio is fixed by the lengths of four bars and by nothing else. In this lesson you learn which lengths set the ratio, and you model a pantograph in FreeCAD in which one number, the scaling ratio, sizes every bar. #FreeCAD #Pantograph #MotionScaling #SimilarTriangles

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Explain with similar triangles why a pantograph copies at one fixed ratio
2. Use spreadsheet formulas so that one ratio $k$ sets several lengths
3. Use a master sketch to give several parts the same hinge points
4. Count the degrees of freedom of a linkage, and of a sketch
5. Test the scaling ratio in the assembly and after a change of $k$

### Real-World Applications

Pantographs copy patterns in engraving and routing machines, enlarge and reduce drawings, and scale the motion of a hand down to small movements in some manipulators.

### The Engineering Problem

> **Design Challenge:** A stylus follows a path. Make a pen draw the same path at another size, with no change of shape.

## Mechanism Fundamentals

<InArticleAd />


### Pantograph Principle

The pantograph of this lesson has four bars on a fixed base:

```text
                              P  pen (output)
                             ╱
                            ╱
       A ─────── I ─────── Q
      ╱       stylus       ╱
     ╱        (input)     ╱
    O ───────────────── B
    fixed pivot
```

- **O** is the fixed pivot on the base. **Link OA** and **Link OB** both turn about it.
- The **input arm** runs from A through I to Q. It is hinged to Link OA at A and carries the stylus at I.
- The **output arm** runs from B through Q to P. It is hinged to Link OB at B and carries the pen at P.
- **Q** is the hinge that joins the two arms.
- O, A, Q and B are the corners of a parallelogram: $AQ = OB$ and $BQ = OA$.
- No link joins A and B. The part "Creating Link AB" shows that such a link locks the mechanism.

The lengths follow one rule. With a base length $L$ (BaseLength, 100 mm) and a scaling ratio $k$ (ScalingRatio, 2):

$$
OA = AI = BQ = L \qquad OB = AQ = BP = k \times L
$$

With these lengths the pen P is always on the straight line from O through the stylus I, and $k$ times as far from O:

$$
\frac{OP}{OI} = k
$$

This holds in every position, so P draws the path of I, $k$ times larger.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Why It Works">

    A parallelogram keeps its opposite sides parallel while it changes shape. So AI stays parallel to OB, and BP stays parallel to OA. Now compare triangle OAI with triangle PBO:

    - The angle at A equals the angle at B, because their sides are parallel in pairs.
    - The sides around these angles have the same ratio: $OB / AI = k$ and $BP / OA = k$.

    Two triangles with an equal angle between sides in the same ratio are similar, so the third sides have that ratio too: $OP = k \times OI$. The equal angles of the two triangles also put P on the line from O through I.

    The same result with vectors: $\vec{OI} = \vec{OA} + \tfrac{1}{k}\,\vec{OB}$ and $\vec{OP} = \vec{OB} + k\,\vec{OA} = k\,\vec{OI}$.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Scaling Factor">

    - **$k > 1$ enlarges.** With $k = 2$ the pen moves twice as far as the stylus.
    - **$k = 1$ cannot be built.** The points I, Q and P become one point, so the stylus and the pen would need the same hole.
    - **To reduce,** build with $k = 2$ and exchange the two tools: the stylus at P and the pen at I give a ratio of 0.5.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Mobility">

    A degree of freedom is one independent way in which a mechanism can move. Grübler's equation counts them for a flat mechanism with $n$ bodies (the base included) and $j$ pin joints. Each free body has 3 (two directions and a rotation), and each pin removes 2. Here $n = 5$ (base, two links, two arms) and $j = 5$ (two at O, one each at A, B and Q):

    $$
    M = 3(n - 1) - 2j = 3(5 - 1) - 2(5) = 2
    $$

    Two degrees of freedom is what a drawing tool needs: the stylus can move left and right, and up and down.

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Part 2: Parametric Design Strategy

<InArticleAd />


<BionicText method="advanced" intensity="medium" class="highlight">
The pantograph is our first ratio-driven mechanism where geometric relationships are more complex. We'll use a master sketch to define the kinematic layout, then reference it from all parts. This ensures geometric consistency and simplifies the parametric control to just two values: base length and scaling ratio.
</BionicText>

### Our Design Approach

<Card title="🎯 Ratio-Based Control Philosophy" icon="rocket">

We'll control the **entire pantograph mechanism** with just two parameters:

**BaseLength** = 100 mm
**ScalingRatio** = 2

All other dimensions calculated automatically using expressions!

$$\text{InputLength} = \text{BaseLength} \times \text{ScalingRatio}$$

$$\text{OutputLength} = \text{BaseLength} \times \text{ScalingRatio}$$

$$\text{CrossLink} = \text{BaseLength} \times \text{ScalingRatio}$$

The short dimensions ($OA$, $AI$, $BQ$) are equal to BaseLength. The long dimensions ($AQ$, $BP$, $OB$) are InputLength, OutputLength and CrossLink.

This is parametric design at its most powerful.

</Card>

### Design Workflow

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Components">

    **Our Pantograph Configuration:**

    - Fixed pivot O at origin
    - Scaling ratio $k = 2:1$ (output is $2\times$ input)
    - Input stylus at point I
    - Output tracer at point P
    - O, I and P always on one straight line, with $OP = k \times OI$

    **Link Naming:**

    1. **Base** - Fixed ground (mounting plate)
    2. **Link OA** - From fixed pivot O to moving pivot A (length BaseLength)
    3. **Link OB** - From fixed pivot O to moving pivot B (length CrossLink)
    4. **Input Arm** - From A through input point I to hinge Q (length InputLength)
    5. **Output Arm** - From B through hinge Q to output point P (length OutputLength)

    There is no link between A and B. Part 7 shows why.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Build Sequence">

    <Steps>

    1. Create Spreadsheet (parameters and formulas)

    2. Create Master Sketch (kinematic layout)

    3. Create Link OA

    4. Create Link OB

    5. Check the mobility (why there is no Link AB)

    6. Create Input Arm (A to Q, with input point I)

    7. Create Output Arm (B to P, with hinge Q)

    8. Create Base

    9. Assemble with constraints

    10. Test parametric control

    </Steps>

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Why Master Sketch?">

    :::note[Master Sketch Benefits]
    **For Complex Mechanisms like Pantographs:**
    - Multiple parts share exact geometric points
    - Similar triangle relationships must be maintained
    - Master sketch ensures all parts stay coordinated
    - Change one sketch, all parts update correctly

    **Think of it as:** The master sketch is the kinematic blueprint; individual parts are the physical implementation.
    :::

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Part 4: Creating the Master Sketch

<InArticleAd />


### Design Intent

<Card title="🗺️ Master Sketch Purpose" icon="document">

The master sketch defines the **kinematic layout** of the pantograph:

- All critical points (O, A, B, I, Q, P)
- All link lengths with parametric control
- Geometric relationships between components

Think of it as the "blueprint" that all parts will reference.

</Card>

### Step-by-Step: Master Sketch

<Steps>

1. **Create standalone sketch**

   - Ensure you're in **Part Design workbench**
   - Click **Create Sketch** (don't create a Body first!)
   - Select **XY_Plane**
   - This sketch won't belong to any body - it's purely reference geometry

</Steps>

### Drawing the Kinematic Layout

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Fixed Pivot O">

    **The center of rotation:**

    The origin $(0, 0)$ is our fixed pivot point O.

    No need to draw anything - the origin marker is already there!

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Link OA">

    **First base link:**

    <Steps>

    1. **Line tool** (press L)
    2. Click at **origin** $(0, 0)$
    3. Draw at approximately $45^\circ$ upward-right
    4. Click to place endpoint
    5. Press **Escape**

    6. **Add length constraint:**
       - Distance/Length constraint tool
       - Click the line
       - Click **ƒx** button
       - Type: `Spreadsheet.BaseLength`
       - Enter

    7. **Add angle constraint:**
       - Angle constraint tool
       - Click the line
       - Type: `45` (degrees from horizontal)
       - Enter

    </Steps>

    Label this endpoint mentally as "A"

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Link OB">

    **Second base link:**

    <Steps>

    1. **Line tool**
    2. Click at **origin**
    3. Draw horizontally to the right
    4. Click to place endpoint
    5. Escape

    6. **Make horizontal:**
       - Select the line
       - Press **H** key (or apply Horizontal constraint)

    7. **Add length constraint:**
       - Distance constraint
       - Click the line
       - ƒx → `Spreadsheet.CrossLink`
       - Enter

    </Steps>

    Label this endpoint as "B"

    OA and OB are two sides of the parallelogram. With $k = 2$, OB is 200 mm and OA is 100 mm.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Input Arm AQ">

    **The arm that carries the input point:**

    The input arm starts at A and is parallel to OB. Draw it as two lines, A to I and I to Q, so that the sketch has a point at I.

    <Steps>

    1. **Line tool**
    2. Start at **A** (endpoint of OA)
    3. Draw horizontally to the right
    4. Click to place endpoint (this is point I)
    5. Start a second line at **I** and draw horizontally to the right
    6. Click to place endpoint (this is point Q), then Escape

    7. **Make both lines horizontal:**
       - Select line AI, press **H**
       - Select line IQ, press **H**
       - Both lines are now parallel to OB and on one straight line

    8. **Length A to I:**
       - Distance constraint
       - Click line AI
       - ƒx → `Spreadsheet.BaseLength`
       - Enter

    9. **Total distance A to Q:**
       - Distance constraint
       - Click **point A**
       - Click **far endpoint** (point Q)
       - ƒx → `Spreadsheet.InputLength`
       - Enter

    </Steps>

    Point I is the input point. With $k = 2$ it is at the middle of AQ.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Closing Side BQ">

    **The fourth side of the parallelogram:**

    <Steps>

    1. **Line tool**
    2. Click on point **B** (endpoint of OB)
    3. Click on point **Q** (far endpoint of the input arm)
    4. Escape

    </Steps>

    Both ends of this line are coincident with points that are already fixed. **Do not add a length or an angle to this line.**

    :::tip[Why No Dimension Here?]
    AQ is parallel to OB and has the same length ($k \times \text{BaseLength}$). So OAQB is already a parallelogram, and BQ is already parallel to OA with length BaseLength. One more dimension on BQ would be a redundant constraint.
    :::

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Output Arm BP">

    **Extension to output point:**

    The output arm runs from B through Q to P. The part from B to Q is the line you just drew. Now add the extension from Q to P.

    <Steps>

    1. **Line tool**
    2. Start at **Q**
    3. Draw upward-right, continuing the direction of line BQ
    4. Click to place endpoint
    5. Escape

    6. **Add angle constraint:**
       - Angle constraint tool
       - Click the new line
       - Type: `45` (the same angle as OA, so the line continues BQ)
       - Enter

    7. **Total distance B to P:**
       - Distance constraint
       - Click **point B**
       - Click **far endpoint** (point P)
       - ƒx → `Spreadsheet.OutputLength`
       - Enter

    </Steps>

    Point P is the output point. Check it: O, I and P are on one straight line.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Construction Mode">

    **Make everything reference geometry:**

    <Steps>

    1. Press **Ctrl+A** to select all geometry

    2. Press **G** to toggle construction mode

    3. All lines turn blue/dashed - they're now construction geometry

    </Steps>

    :::tip[Why Construction Mode?]
    Construction geometry is **reference only** - it won't be extruded into 3D. It's perfect for master sketches that define layout but aren't physical parts themselves.
    :::

    **Optional:** Add small circles (radius 2mm) at points O, A, B, I, Q, P for visualization

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Verify Constraints">

    **Check the solver:**

    Should show: **"Fully constrained"**

    **What's defined:**
    - O is at origin (coincident)
    - OA length and angle ($45^\circ$)
    - OB length and horizontal
    - AI length and horizontal
    - IQ horizontal, and total distance A to Q
    - QP angle ($45^\circ$), and total distance B to P
    - BQ: coincident at both ends, no dimension

    **Count the degrees of freedom:**

    | Item | Count | Degrees of freedom |
    |------|-------|--------------------|
    | Lines (OA, OB, AI, IQ, BQ, QP) | 6 | $6 \times 4 = +24$ |
    | Coincident points (O twice, A, I, B, Q twice) | 7 | $7 \times 2 = -14$ |
    | Horizontal (OB, AI, IQ) | 3 | $-3$ |
    | Angle (OA, QP) | 2 | $-2$ |
    | Length or distance (OA, OB, AI, AQ, BP) | 5 | $-5$ |
    | **Remaining** | | $24 - 14 - 3 - 2 - 5 = 0$ |

    The **sketch** has zero degrees of freedom because it shows the pantograph in one fixed position. The angle constraints and the horizontal constraints fix this position. The **assembly** in Part 11 does not have these constraints, so it keeps two degrees of freedom and can move.

    With the default values the points are at $O = (0, 0)$, $A = (70.7, 70.7)$, $B = (200, 0)$, $I = (170.7, 70.7)$, $Q = (270.7, 70.7)$ and $P = (341.4, 141.4)$, in mm. Notice that $P = 2 \times I$.

    :::caution[If Not Fully Constrained]
    Missing constraints might include:
    - Origin coincident with O
    - Horizontal constraint on OB, AI or IQ
    - Angle constraint on OA or QP
    - Distance A to Q or distance B to P

    If the solver reports a redundant constraint, remove any length or angle that you added to line BQ.
    :::

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

<Steps>

1. **Close the sketch**

   Click **Close** button in toolbar

2. **Rename for clarity**

   - Find "Sketch" in the tree
   - Right-click → Rename → `MasterSketch`

</Steps>

**Master sketch complete!** This defines all key points for the entire mechanism.

<InArticleAd />


### Design Intent

<Card title="⚙️ Link OA Requirements" icon="document">

Link from fixed pivot O to moving pivot A:

- **Holes at O and A** for pin joints

### Step-by-Step: Link OA

<Steps>

1. **Create Body**

   - Part Design workbench
   - Click **Create Body** button
   - Right-click → Rename → `Link_OA`

   - Select Link_OA body
   - Create Sketch → **XY_Plane**

   - Click **External Geometry** tool (or press E)
   - In the tree, find and select **MasterSketch**
   - Click on points **O** and **A** in the 3D view
   - They appear as **purple reference points**

   :::tip[External Geometry Magic]
   These purple points are **linked** to the master sketch! If the master sketch updates, these references update automatically. This is how we maintain geometric consistency across all parts.
   :::

4. **Draw the link profile**

   - **Circle** at point O: Radius constraint → ƒx → `Spreadsheet.PinRadius`
   - **Circle** at point A: Radius constraint → ƒx → `Spreadsheet.PinRadius`
   - **Rectangle** connecting them, symmetric about the OA line
   - **Width** dimension: ƒx → `Spreadsheet.LinkWidth`

5. **Close sketch**

6. **Pad the link**

   - Select the sketch
   - Click **Pad** tool
   - Length: ƒx → `Spreadsheet.LinkThickness`
   - OK

</Steps>

**Link OA complete!**

## Part 7: Creating Link AB

<InArticleAd />


A working pantograph has **no link between A and B**. Do not create one. This part shows why, with a mobility count.

### Counting the Degrees of Freedom

Grübler's equation gives the mobility $M$ of a planar mechanism with $n$ bodies (the base included) and $j$ pin joints:

$$M = 3(n - 1) - 2j$$

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Without Link AB">

    **Bodies:** Base, Link_OA, Link_OB, InputArm, OutputArm, so $n = 5$.

    **Pin joints:** Link_OA to Base at O, Link_OB to Base at O, InputArm to Link_OA at A, OutputArm to Link_OB at B, InputArm to OutputArm at Q, so $j = 5$.

    $$M = 3(5 - 1) - 2(5) = 2$$

    ✓ Two degrees of freedom. The input point I can move in $x$ and in $y$, and P follows it.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="With Link AB">

    **Bodies:** one more, so $n = 6$.

    **Pin joints:** two more (Link AB to Link_OA at A, Link AB to Link_OB at B), so $j = 7$.

    $$M = 3(6 - 1) - 2(7) = 1$$

    Only one degree of freedom remains. O, A and B now form a rigid triangle, so the parallelogram cannot change shape. The whole mechanism can only turn about O as one rigid piece. It cannot scale a drawing.

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

:::tip[Design Rule]
Each hinge joins two bars with one pin. No bar is pinned twice to the same bar. Two pins between the same two bars make them one rigid body.
:::

**Mobility check complete!** The pantograph needs four moving parts, not five.

## Part 8: Creating Input Arm (OI)

<InArticleAd />


### Design Intent

<Card title="📍 Input Arm Purpose" icon="puzzle">

This arm carries the input point I (input stylus). It does not touch O. It runs from A through I to Q:

- **Pivot at A** (joins Link OA)
- **Hole at I** (input point for user to control), at distance BaseLength from A
- **Pivot at Q** (joins the Output Arm), at distance InputLength from A
- **Stylus holder** at point I

</Card>

### Step-by-Step: Input Arm

<Steps>

1. **Body:** Create and rename to `InputArm`

2. **Sketch on XY_Plane**

3. **External Geometry:**
   - Reference MasterSketch
   - Select points A, I, and Q

4. **Draw arm profile:**

   - **Long rectangle** from A to Q
   - **Holes at A and Q** for pin joints (PinRadius)
   - **Width:** LinkWidth (or slightly narrower if needed)
   - **Stylus holder at I:** Small protrusion or marking hole

   :::tip[Design Tip]
   You might want to make this arm slightly narrower than LinkWidth to avoid interference with other links. Try LinkWidth - 2mm.
   :::

5. **Pad:** LinkThickness

</Steps>

With the default values the holes are at 0 mm (A), 100 mm (I) and 200 mm (Q) along the arm.

**Input Arm complete!**

## Part 9: Creating Output Arm (OP)

<InArticleAd />


This arm carries the output point P (output tracer). It does not touch O. It runs from B through Q to P.

### Step-by-Step: Output Arm

<Steps>

1. **Body:** `OutputArm`

2. **Sketch on XY_Plane**

3. **External Geometry:**
   - Reference B, Q, and P

4. **Draw arm profile:**

   - **Rectangle from B to P**
   - **Holes at B and Q** (PinRadius)
   - **Width:** LinkWidth (or narrower)
   - **Tracer mount at P:** Protruding pin or marking feature

5. **Pad:** LinkThickness

</Steps>

With the default values the holes are at 0 mm (B), 100 mm (Q) and 200 mm (P) along the arm. The hole spacing is the same as on the Input Arm.

**Output Arm complete!**

<InArticleAd />


### Design Intent

<Card title="⚓ Base Requirements" icon="document">

Fixed mounting plate providing:

<Steps>

## Part 11: Assembly

<InArticleAd />


<BionicText method="advanced" intensity="medium" class="highlight">
Assembly is where the pantograph comes to life! With five separate parts and five hinges, proper constraint strategy is critical. We'll use axial alignment to create one revolute joint at each hinge while maintaining the parallelogram geometry.
</BionicText>

### Assembly Strategy

<Card title="🎯 Assembly Constraints Plan" icon="rocket">

1. **Base:** Fixed (ground link)
2. **Two links pivot at O:** Link_OA, Link_OB
3. **Joint at A:** Link_OA and InputArm meet
4. **Joint at B:** Link_OB and OutputArm meet
5. **Joint at Q:** InputArm and OutputArm meet
6. **All motion in XY plane**

Five joints in total. Each joint connects two parts once. This leaves two degrees of freedom, as calculated in Part 7.

</Card>

:::note[Two Levels]
Parallel bars can share a level. Put Link_OA and OutputArm on the lower level, and Link_OB and InputArm on the upper level. Then the two parts at every hinge are on different levels and no bar collides with another.
:::

### Creating the Assembly

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Setup">

    <Steps>

    1. **Switch to Assembly workbench**

       Use workbench dropdown

    2. **Create new assembly**

       Assembly → Create Assembly

    3. **Add all parts**

       Drag from tree or use "Add Part" button:
       - Base
       - Link_OA
       - Link_OB
       - InputArm
       - OutputArm

    </Steps>

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Fixing Base">

    <Steps>

    1. Select **Base** in assembly tree

    2. Click **Fixed constraint** button

    3. Base is now locked in place (ground link)

    </Steps>

    :::note[Why Fix the Base?]
    Every mechanism needs a ground reference. The base provides this fixed reference point from which all other motion is measured. Point O on the base becomes our fixed pivot.
    :::

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Central Pivot at O">

    **Two links pivot at O:**

    <Steps>

    1. **Link_OA to Base:**
       - Select Link_OA hole axis at O
       - Select Base hole axis at O
       - **Axial Align** constraint

    2. **Link_OB to Base:**
       - Link_OB hole at O → Base hole at O
       - Axial Align

    </Steps>

    Both links can now rotate about O! The two arms do not connect to O.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Joint at A">

    **Two parts meet at point A:**

    <Steps>

    1. **InputArm to Link_OA:**
       - InputArm hole at A → Link_OA hole at A
       - Axial Align

    </Steps>

    Point A is now a complete pin joint!

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Joint at B">

    **Two parts meet at point B:**

    <Steps>

    1. **OutputArm to Link_OB:**
       - OutputArm hole at B → Link_OB hole at B
       - Axial Align

    </Steps>

    Point B is now a complete pin joint!

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Joint at Q">

    **The two arms meet at point Q:**

    <Steps>

    1. **InputArm to OutputArm:**
       - InputArm hole at Q → OutputArm hole at Q
       - Axial Align

    </Steps>

    Point Q closes the parallelogram OAQB. This is the fifth and last joint.

    :::caution[One Joint per Hinge]
    Do not add a second Axial Align between two parts that are already joined. Two pins between the same two parts lock them together and the pantograph stops working.
    :::

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Testing Motion">

    **Test the pantograph:**

    <Steps>

    1. **Try dragging the input arm** (at point I)

    2. **Observe the output arm** (at point P)

    3. **Verify scaling:**
       - Input moves 10mm → Output should move 20mm
       - Ratio should be 2:1
       - O, I and P should stay on one straight line

    </Steps>

    :::tip[Troubleshooting Motion]
    **If mechanism won't move:**
    - Check that only Base is Fixed
    - All pin joints should be Axial Align (not Fixed!)
    - Remove any redundant constraints (five joints only, and no Link AB)
    - Links may need Z-offset to avoid interference

    **If parts interfere:**
    - Link_OB and InputArm may need to be offset in Z (the upper level)
    - Or make them narrower (LinkWidth - 2mm)
    :::

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Part 12: Verifying the Scaling Ratio

<InArticleAd />


<BionicText method="advanced" intensity="medium" class="highlight">
The true test of a pantograph is its scaling accuracy. We'll verify both the geometric construction and the parametric control to ensure the mechanism works correctly at any scaling ratio.
</BionicText>

### Verification Methods

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Manual Test">

    **Measure actual motion:**

    <Steps>

    1. **Mark starting position**

       - Note coordinates of input point I
       - Note coordinates of output point P
       - Record or screenshot

    2. **Move input 10mm horizontally**

       - Drag input arm to move I exactly 10mm right
       - Use FreeCAD measurement tools if needed

    3. **Measure output displacement**

       - Check new position of P
       - Calculate displacement
       - Should be 20mm ($2\times$ input) with $k = 2$

    4. **Test vertical motion**

       - Move I 10mm upward
       - P should move 20mm upward

    </Steps>

    ✅ **Success indicator:** Output displacement = $k \times$ Input displacement

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Parametric Test">

    **Change the scaling ratio:**

    <Steps>

    1. **Open Spreadsheet**

       Double-click Spreadsheet in tree

    2. **Change ScalingRatio**

       - Click cell B2
       - Change from `2` to `3`
       - Press Enter

    3. **Recompute**

       Press **Ctrl+R** or click Recompute button

    4. **Observe automatic updates:**

       - InputLength now = 300mm ($100 \times 3$)
       - OutputLength now = 300mm ($100 \times 3$)
       - CrossLink now = 300mm
       - Link OA and the distances AI and BQ stay at 100mm
       - All parts update geometry!
       - Assembly adjusts automatically!

    5. **Test motion again**

       - Move input 10mm
       - Output should now move 30mm ($3\times$ input)

    </Steps>

    ✅ **This is parametric design power!**

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Mathematical Verification">

    **Verify the math:**

    For a pantograph with scaling ratio $k$:

    ```
    If input point I is at (x, y) relative to O,
    Then output point P is at (k×x, k×y) relative to O
    ```

    **Example with $k = 2$:**
    - Input moves to $I = (50, 50)\ \text{mm}$
    - Output should be at $P = (100, 100)\ \text{mm}$

    **Why:** $\vec{OI} = \vec{OA} + \tfrac{1}{k}\,\vec{OB}$ and $\vec{OP} = \vec{OB} + k\,\vec{OA}$, so $\vec{OP} = k\,\vec{OI}$ in every position.

    **Test this:**

    <Steps>
    1. Position input at $I = (50, 50)$
    2. Measure $P$ coordinates
    3. Verify $P = (100, 100)$
    </Steps>

    :::tip[Perfect Geometry]
    If your measurements match the math, your pantograph is geometrically perfect! The similar triangle relationships are working correctly.
    :::

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Part 14: Testing Parametric Control

<InArticleAd />


<BionicText method="advanced" intensity="medium" class="highlight">
This is where expression-based parametric design really shines. Watch as a single parameter change recalculates all dependent dimensions and updates the entire mechanism automatically.
</BionicText>

### Parametric Experiments

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Experiment 1: k = 1.5">

    **Create a small enlargement, close to a 1:1 copy:**

    <Steps>

    1. **Open Spreadsheet**

    2. **Change ScalingRatio**
       - Cell B2: Type `1.5`
       - Enter

    3. **Recompute** (Ctrl+R)

    4. **Observe:**
       - InputLength = 150mm ($100 \times 1.5$)
       - OutputLength = 150mm
       - CrossLink = 150mm
       - On each arm the last two holes are now only 50mm apart

    5. **Test motion:**
       - Input 10mm → Output 15mm
       - 1.5:1 scaling ratio

    </Steps>

    :::note[Why Not k = 1?]
    As $k$ approaches 1, the points I, Q and P move together. At exactly $k = 1$ they are one point, so the stylus and the pen would need the same hole. This pantograph cannot make a 1:1 copy. Keep ScalingRatio greater than 1.
    :::

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Experiment 2: k = 3">

    **Create a $3\times$ enlargement:**

    <Steps>

    1. **Change ScalingRatio to 3**

    2. **Recompute**

    3. **Observe:**
       - InputLength = 300mm
       - OutputLength = 300mm ($3\times$ BaseLength)
       - CrossLink = 300mm
       - Mechanism is larger

    4. **Test motion:**
       - Input 10mm → Output 30mm
       - 3:1 scaling ratio

    </Steps>

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Experiment 3: Reduction">

    **Create a $0.5\times$ reduction:**

    A pantograph works in both directions. To reduce, exchange the stylus and the pen.

    <Steps>

    1. **Keep ScalingRatio at 2**

    2. **Recompute**

    3. **Exchange the roles:**
       - Point P is now the input (stylus)
       - Point I is now the output (pen)
       - The ratio is $1/k = 1/2 = 0.5$

    4. **Test motion:**
       - Move P 10mm → I moves 5mm
       - Perfect reduction pantograph

    </Steps>

    :::tip[Reduction Applications]
    Reduction pantographs are used for miniaturization, engraving small text from large templates, and precision scaling down.
    :::

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Experiment 4: Scale Up">

    **Change base size:**

    <Steps>

    1. **Reset ScalingRatio to 2**

    2. **Change BaseLength**
       - Cell B3: Type `150`
       - Enter

    3. **Recompute**

    4. **Observe:**
       - Entire mechanism scales up proportionally
       - InputLength = 300mm ($150 \times 2$)
       - OutputLength = 300mm ($150 \times 2$)
       - CrossLink = 300mm
       - Link OA and the distances AI and BQ = 150mm
       - Scaling ratio remains $k = 2$

    </Steps>

    **This demonstrates the power of two-parameter control!**

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Common Issues and Solutions

<InArticleAd />


### ``Output doesn't scale correctly"

- **Cause:** Incorrect link length ratios
- **Solution:** Verify expressions in spreadsheet
- **Fix:** InputLength, OutputLength and CrossLink must each = BaseLength $\times\ k$
- **Fix:** Distance AI must = BaseLength, so that O, I and P are on one line

### ``Mechanism binds or locks up"

- **Cause:** Links interfering, or over-constrained assembly (a link between A and B, or two joints between the same two parts)
- **Solution:** Check clearances, ensure only Base is Fixed, and count five joints
- **Fix:** May need to offset arms in Z-direction

### ``Scaling ratio parameter doesn't update links"

- **Cause:** Dimensions not using formulas
- **Solution:** Check that InputLength cell has `=B3*B2` not a hardcoded value
- **Fix:** Recreate spreadsheet formulas

### ``Parts misalign in assembly"

- **Cause:** Not referencing master sketch consistently
- **Solution:** All part sketches must use External Geometry from same master sketch
- **Fix:** Rebuild parts referencing master sketch points
