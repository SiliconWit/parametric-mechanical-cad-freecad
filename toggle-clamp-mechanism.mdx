A workpiece that moves during welding or drilling is scrap, and sometimes an injury. A screw clamp holds firmly but is slow to close. A spring clamp is fast but weak. A toggle clamp is fast and firm: one push of the handle gives a large clamping force, and the clamp stays shut with no hand on it. It does this with four bars and four pins that go "over center": two bars come into one straight line and then a little past it. In this lesson you calculate where that line falls for given bar lengths, and then model the clamp in FreeCAD so that every size comes from one table of values. #FreeCAD #ToggleClamp #OverCenter #SelfLocking

:::tip[Simulate before you build]
Before modeling, try your geometry in the [Toggle Clamp Mechanism Simulator](/product-development/toggle-clamp-mechanism-simulator/) to check the over-center force amplification, the lock margin, and the pin and link stresses. For structured experiments with Python analysis, see the [Mechanism Design and Simulation](/education/mechanism-design-simulation/) course.
:::

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Find the dead center angle of an over-center linkage from its link lengths
2. Build parts whose sizes all come from one spreadsheet of named values
3. Explain why the force at the clamp arm rises steeply near dead center
4. Set the open and the locked position of the mechanism with angles
5. Make a drawing that shows the mechanism in both positions

### Real-World Applications

Toggle clamps hold parts in welding jigs, on drilling and milling machines, in woodworking, and in test fixtures that press a circuit board onto its test pins.

### The Engineering Problem

> **Design Challenge:** Hold a workpiece with a large force from a small push of the hand, and keep it held after the hand lets go.

### Over-Center Mechanism Principle

Stand with your knees bent and your muscles must work to hold you up. Straighten your knees and the bones carry your weight with almost no effort. Two bars that come into one straight line behave in the same way. A knee stops at straight, but this linkage is made to go a little past it.

The clamp of this lesson has four links joined by pins:

- The **base** is fixed. It carries two pivots 40 mm apart: O for the handle and Q for the clamp arm.
- The **handle** turns about O. Its far hole C is 120 mm from O.
- The **main link** is 80 mm long. It joins C on the handle to P on the clamp arm.
- The **clamp arm** turns about Q. Hole P is 30 mm from Q, and the pad that touches the work is 100 mm from Q.

```text
 Open position, handle 45° above horizontal (not to scale)

                         C
                       ╱ │
        handle       ╱   │  main link
        120 mm     ╱     │  80 mm
                 ╱       │
               ╱         │
   O ─────── Q ───────── P ─────────────► pad
   │← 40 mm →│←  30 mm  →│
       base              clamp arm, pad 100 mm from Q
```

As you push the handle down, the mechanism passes through three states:

1. **Before center (open).** The handle and the main link make a clear angle at C. The clamp arm moves fast, with little force.
2. **At dead center.** The handle and the main link lie in one straight line. The clamp arm is at the end of its travel, and the force it can give is at its largest.
3. **Past center (locked).** The handle goes a few degrees further, to a stop. The push from the work now tries to move the handle further closed, against the stop, so the clamp cannot open by itself.

## What You'll Build

<InArticleAd />


You will build a spreadsheet of named values, four parts (base, handle, main link, clamp arm), an assembly with four pins, and a drawing that shows the clamp open and locked.

The model is **parametric**: a size is typed once, as a named value in the spreadsheet, and each sketch refers to that name. Change the value and every part that uses it follows. Without this, one changed length means editing each sketch by hand, and one forgotten sketch gives parts that no longer fit together.

### Understanding Workbenches for This Project

FreeCAD groups its tools into **workbenches**, chosen with the dropdown in the top toolbar. This lesson uses Spreadsheet (the table of values), Sketcher (flat outlines), Part Design (solid parts), Assembly (parts joined by pins) and TechDraw (the drawing on paper).

A **constraint** is a rule that you attach to a sketch: this line is horizontal, this hole is 40 mm from that one. FreeCAD moves the lines until every rule is true, so you draw roughly and the rules give the exact shape.

## Part 2: Parametric Design Strategy

<InArticleAd />


The angles of this clamp cannot be chosen freely. Once the link lengths are fixed, geometry decides where dead center is, and the closed angle must lie a few degrees past it. A guessed closed angle either stops before dead center, so the clamp springs open, or cannot be reached at all.

### Linkage Analysis

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Critical Positions">

**Where is dead center?** At dead center the main link lies back along the handle, so hole P is on the handle line, $120 - 80 = 40$ mm from O. Hole P is also always 30 mm from Q. So O, Q and P make a triangle with three known sides, and its angle at O is the handle angle at dead center, $\theta_{dc}$.

The cosine rule gives an angle of a triangle from its three sides. With $OP = 40$ mm, $OQ = 40$ mm and $QP = 30$ mm:

$$
\cos\theta_{dc} = \frac{OP^2 + OQ^2 - QP^2}{2 \times OP \times OQ} = \frac{40^2 + 40^2 - 30^2}{2 \times 40 \times 40} = \frac{2300}{3200} = 0.719
$$

So $\theta_{dc} = 44.0^\circ$. The handle moves down to reach it, so in the model the dead center angle is about -44°.

- **Open position:** handle 45° above horizontal, about 34° away from the main link. Clamp arm about 12° above horizontal, clear of the work.
- **Locked position:** handle at about -49°, which is -44° and 5° more. The clamp arm has swung down through about 124° to about -112°. The pad is then about 93 mm below the line of the pivots, past the edge of the base plate. The work sits there.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Parameter Strategy">

Two distances are typed straight into the sketches as plain numbers: the 40 mm between the pivots and the 30 mm from Q to hole P. Both decide where dead center is, so the calculation holds only while they keep these values.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Part 3: Creating the Parameter Spreadsheet

<InArticleAd />


### Building Your Parameter Table

The spreadsheet is the one place where sizes are typed, so build it before any sketch.

<Steps>

1. **Switch to the Spreadsheet workbench** with the workbench dropdown.

2. **Insert a spreadsheet:** Spreadsheet → Create Spreadsheet. An object named "Spreadsheet" appears in the tree on the left.

3. **Open it** with a double-click on "Spreadsheet" in the tree.

</Steps>

### Entering Parameters

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Parameter Table">

Type this table. Column A is the name, column B the value, column C the unit. The closed angle in B13 is the dead center angle of -44° and the over-center angle of B14: $-44 - 5 = -49$.

| Row | A | B | C | Meaning |
|-----|---|---|---|---------|
| **1** | Parameter | Value | Unit | Headers |
| **2** | HandleLength | 120 | mm | Handle, O to C |
| **3** | MainLinkLength | 80 | mm | Main link, C to P |
| **4** | ClampArmLength | 100 | mm | Clamp arm, Q to the pad |
| **5** | LinkWidth | 25 | mm | Width of all links |
| **6** | LinkThickness | 8 | mm | Thickness of all links |
| **7** | PinDiameter | 8 | mm | Pivot pins |
| **8** | PinRadius | =B7/2 | mm | Formula |
| **9** | BaseWidth | 120 | mm | Base plate |
| **10** | BaseHeight | 40 | mm | Base plate |
| **11** | BaseThickness | 15 | mm | Base plate |
| **12** | HandleAngleOpen | 45 | deg | Open position |
| **13** | HandleAngleClosed | -49 | deg | Locked position |
| **14** | OverCenterAngle | 5 | deg | Angle past dead center |

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Creating Aliases">

An **alias** is a name that you give to a cell. A sketch can then ask for `Spreadsheet.HandleLength` in place of cell B2. The name says what the value is, and it still points to the right value if you add a row later.

Give an alias to every value cell:

<Steps>

1. Click cell **B2**
2. Right-click → **Properties**
3. In "Alias" field, type: `HandleLength`
4. Click **OK**
5. Repeat for B3 through B14. The alias of each cell is the name in column A of its row, for example `MainLinkLength` for B3 and `PinRadius` for B8.

</Steps>

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Best Practices">

:::tip[Spreadsheet Best Practices]
- Where one value follows from another, type a formula. B8 holds `=B7/2`, so the pin radius can never disagree with the pin diameter
- The units in column C are notes for the reader. FreeCAD does not calculate with them
- No sketch reads `HandleAngleClosed` or `OverCenterAngle`. They record the design, and you use them when you set the handle in the assembly
:::

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

**Close the spreadsheet** when done (click the Close button).

## Part 4: Creating the Base

<InArticleAd />


The base is the fixed plate that everything else turns on. It needs a pivot hole for the handle (O), a pivot hole for the clamp arm (Q) 40 mm from the first, and holes to bolt it down.

### Step-by-Step: Base Part

Every part is made in the same way: a **Body** (the container for one solid part), then a **sketch** (a flat outline on a plane), then a **Pad**, which pushes the outline out to a thickness.

<Steps>

1. **Create a Body**

   - Go to the **Part Design** workbench and click **New Body**
   - Right-click → Rename → type `Base`

2. **Create a Sketch**

   - Select the "Base" body in the tree and click **New Sketch**
   - When the dialog asks for a plane, select **XY_Plane** and click **OK**

   FreeCAD changes to the **Sketcher** workbench by itself.

</Steps>

### Drawing the Base Profile

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Main Plate">

**Goal: a rectangle centered on the origin, with its size taken from the spreadsheet.**

<Steps>

1. **Rectangle tool:** draw a rectangle around the origin. The size does not matter yet.

2. **Center it:** click the bottom left corner, the top right corner, then the origin, and press **S** (Symmetric). Do not make two edges symmetric about an axis: the Rectangle tool already holds its sides horizontal and vertical, so that repeats a rule, and the solver stops with "redundant constraints".

3. **Width from the spreadsheet:** add a Distance constraint on the width, click the **ƒx** button (it lets you type a name in place of a number), type `Spreadsheet.BaseWidth` and press Enter.

4. **Height from the spreadsheet:** the same, with `Spreadsheet.BaseHeight`.

</Steps>

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Pivot Holes">

**Goal: hole O at the origin and hole Q 40 mm to its right.**

<Steps>

1. **Handle pivot hole O:**

   - Circle tool, click at the **origin**, press Escape
   - Coincident constraint: center → origin
   - Radius constraint: ƒx → `Spreadsheet.PinRadius`

2. **Clamp arm pivot hole Q:**

   - Circle tool, click about 40 mm to the right of the origin, press Escape
   - Horizontal constraint: center → origin
   - Distance from origin: `40` mm, the side OQ of the triangle that gave the dead center angle
   - Radius: ƒx → `Spreadsheet.PinRadius`

3. **Optional mounting holes:** 2 to 4 circles near the corners, radius `5` mm (a 10 mm hole, a loose fit for an M8 bolt), each with a distance of 8 to 10 mm from its two nearest edges.

</Steps>

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Finalize Sketch">

A sketch is **fully constrained** when its rules leave nothing free to move. A sketch with a loose line looks correct today. But when a value in the spreadsheet changes, FreeCAD may move that line anywhere that still obeys the other rules, and the part changes shape with no warning.

<Steps>

1. **Check:** the "Solver Messages" panel must say **"Fully constrained"**, and the sketch turns green. If it reports degrees of freedom, drag each line with the mouse. The one that moves lacks a rule.

2. **Close the sketch** with the **Close** button in the toolbar.

</Steps>

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

### Creating 3D: Pad Operation

<Steps>

1. Select the sketch in the tree (under the Base body).

2. Click the **Pad** tool in the Part Design toolbar.

3. In the Pad panel on the left, set Type to **Dimension** and Length to **ƒx** → `Spreadsheet.BaseThickness`.

4. Click **OK**.

</Steps>

The outline is now a plate 15 mm thick. Press **V** then **F** to fit it in the view.

## Part 5: Creating the Handle

<InArticleAd />


The handle is the lever the hand pushes. It needs a pivot hole at one end (O) and a hole for the main link at the other end (C), HandleLength away.

### Step-by-Step: Handle Part

<Steps>

1. **Create a new Body** in the Part Design workbench and rename it `Handle`.

2. **Create a Sketch:** select the Handle body, then New Sketch → XY_Plane.

</Steps>

### Drawing the Handle Profile

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Centerline Setup">

**Goal: one reference line from O to C that carries the length and the angle of the handle.** The holes and the outline are then hung on this line.

<Steps>

1. **Line tool** (press G, L): click at the **origin**, draw upward and to the right, click to place the endpoint, press **Escape**.

2. **Make it construction geometry:** select the line and press **G, N**. The line turns blue. A construction line is a reference only: it carries dimensions and does not become solid material.

3. **Length:** Distance constraint, click both endpoints of the line, **ƒx** → `Spreadsheet.HandleLength`.

4. **Angle:** Angle constraint, click the **construction line**, then the **horizontal X-axis** (the red line through the origin), **ƒx** → `Spreadsheet.HandleAngleOpen`.

</Steps>

The line is now 120 mm long at 45°, the open position. It is drawn at this angle only so that the sketch has nothing loose. In the assembly the handle turns about its pin.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Holes">

**Goal: one hole at each end of the reference line.**

<Steps>

1. **Pivot hole O:** a circle at the **origin**, with Coincident (center → origin) and Radius ƒx → `Spreadsheet.PinRadius`, as on the base.

2. **Hole C for the main link:** a circle at the **far endpoint** of the construction line, with Coincident (center → line endpoint) and Radius ƒx → `Spreadsheet.PinRadius`.

</Steps>

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Handle Body">

**Goal: an outline around both holes, centered on the reference line.**

<Steps>

1. **Four lines** (G, L), joined end to end, around both circles. Not the Rectangle tool: it holds its edges horizontal and vertical, and this line is at 45°.

2. **Directions:** the two long edges **parallel** (P) to the construction line, the two short edges **perpendicular** (N) to a long edge.

3. **Distances:** select point O, then each long edge and the short edge behind it, and point C, then the short edge beyond it. Each **K, D**, **ƒx** → `Spreadsheet.LinkWidth / 2`. The bar is now LinkWidth wide and reaches 12.5 mm past each hole.

4. **Optional:** draw a wider or rounded shape at the free end as a grip.

</Steps>

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Finalize">

<Steps>

1. **Check that the sketch is fully constrained.** Four joined lines have 8 freedoms; the two parallel and two perpendicular rules remove 4, the four distances the other 4. If the solver still reports some, a distance is missing.

2. **Close the sketch.**

3. **Pad the handle:** Pad tool, Length ƒx → `Spreadsheet.LinkThickness`, OK.

</Steps>

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Part 6: Creating the Main Link

<InArticleAd />


The main link carries the push from the handle to the clamp arm. It is a plain bar with one hole at each end, MainLinkLength apart. This length sets dead center: change it and the 40 mm from O to P at dead center changes with it.

### Quick Creation Steps

The steps are those of the handle, with a horizontal centerline.

<Steps>

1. **Create Body:** Create and rename to `MainLink`

2. **Create Sketch on XY_Plane**

3. **Draw the link profile:**

   **Centerline (construction):**
   - Line tool, start at the origin
   - Draw to the right
   - Make it construction (G, N)
   - Horizontal constraint (select the line and press H)
   - Length: ƒx → `Spreadsheet.MainLinkLength`

   **Holes at both ends:**
   - Circle at origin: Radius = ƒx → `Spreadsheet.PinRadius`
   - Circle at far end: Coincident to endpoint, Radius = ƒx → `Spreadsheet.PinRadius`

   **Link body:**
   - Rectangle around both holes (the Rectangle tool works here: the line is horizontal)
   - Four distances, ƒx → `Spreadsheet.LinkWidth / 2`: the first hole center to the top, bottom and left edges, the second to the right edge

4. **Check:** the sketch must be fully constrained, as for the handle

5. **Close and Pad:** Length = ƒx → `Spreadsheet.LinkThickness`

</Steps>

:::tip[The sketch does not decide the angle]
The link is drawn horizontal only so that its sketch has nothing loose. The angle it takes in the clamp is decided by the two pins of the assembly. A part does not need a loose sketch in order to move.
:::

## Part 7: Creating the Clamp Arm

<InArticleAd />


The clamp arm is the output link. It needs a pivot hole at one end (Q), a hole for the main link (P) 30 mm from the pivot, and a pad at the far end, ClampArmLength from the pivot.

### Step-by-Step: Clamp Arm Part

<Steps>

1. **New Body:** rename it `ClampArm`

2. **Create Sketch on XY_Plane**

</Steps>

### Drawing the Clamp Arm Profile

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Centerline & Holes">

**Goal: a reference line from Q to the pad, with hole P on it.**

<Steps>

1. **Centerline (construction):** a line from the origin to the right, made horizontal (press H) and construction (G, N), with Length ƒx → `Spreadsheet.ClampArmLength`.

2. **Pivot hole at the origin:** a circle at the origin, Radius ƒx → `Spreadsheet.PinRadius`.

3. **Hole P for the main link:**

   - A circle with its center on the line, between the two ends
   - Coincident (point on object): center → the line
   - Distance from origin: `30` mm, the side QP of the triangle that gave the dead center angle
   - Radius: ƒx → `Spreadsheet.PinRadius`

</Steps>

:::note[Why 30 mm]
The clamp arm is a lever about Q. The main link pushes at 30 mm and the pad presses at 100 mm, so the pad moves 3.3 times as far as hole P and presses with 0.3 of the force there. A hole nearer to Q gives the pad more movement and less force, and it also moves dead center.
:::

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Arm Body and Pad">

**Goal: an outline around the holes, with a wider end that spreads the force on the work.**

<Steps>

1. **Arm:** a rectangle around the centerline, with the four distances of the main link (`Spreadsheet.LinkWidth / 2` from the pivot center to the top, bottom and left edges, and from the far end of the centerline to the right edge).

2. **Check that the sketch is fully constrained,** close it and pad it: Pad tool, Length ƒx → `Spreadsheet.LinkThickness`, OK.

3. **Pad block:** a second sketch on XY_Plane, a rectangle 20 mm long and 40 mm across (LinkWidth + 15), centered on the far end of the arm line, 100 mm from Q. Pad it LinkThickness. It overlaps the arm, so the two pads join into one solid, and the block is wider than the arm. Two pads are cleaner than two overlapping rectangles in one sketch, which FreeCAD 1.1 pads with a "Refine failed" warning.

</Steps>

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Part 8: Assembly

<InArticleAd />


So far the four parts lie on top of each other at the origin. The assembly joins them with pins. Each pin lets two parts turn about one shared axis. With the base fixed and four pins in place, one movement remains: turn the handle, and the main link and the clamp arm must follow.

:::note[Which Assembly workbench these steps name]
These steps use the Assembly workbench built into FreeCAD 1.0 and later, run in 1.1. In 0.21 you install an add-on (Tools → Addon manager): in Assembly3 a Revolute joint is Axial Alignment, in A2plus axisCoincident. The plan is the same in every workbench: one fixed part and four pin joints.
:::

### Creating the Assembly

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Setup and Base">

<Steps>

1. **Switch to the Assembly workbench** with the workbench dropdown.

2. **Create a new assembly:** Assembly → **New Assembly**

3. **Add the parts:** **Insert Component** (**I**): Base, Handle, MainLink and ClampArm.

4. **Ground the base:** select **Base** and click **Toggle Grounded** (**G**). Movement is measured from something that stands still. Without a fixed part, a drag on the handle moves the whole clamp across the screen.

</Steps>

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Four Pins">

Each pin is a joint between two circular hole edges. Pick the edge on the **front** face of the part that is already placed, then the edge on the **back** face (the sketch plane side) of the part that comes on top of it. So the parts stand in layers: base, handle, main link, clamp arm. In one layer the handle passes through the clamp arm near dead center, because hole P then lies on the handle line.

<Steps>

1. **Pin at O:** **Revolute Joint** (**R**): Base hole at the origin, then the Handle pivot hole. The handle now turns about O.

2. **Pin at C:** Revolute: Handle far hole, then MainLink hole 1.

3. **Pin at P:** Revolute: MainLink hole 2 (front face), then the ClampArm 30 mm hole. The clamp arm now lies on top of the main link.

4. **Pin at Q:** **Cylindrical Joint** (**C**): Base second pivot hole, 40 mm from the origin, then the ClampArm pivot hole. This is the last pin of the loop; the clamp arm stands 16 mm above the base here, so a real clamp has a spacer on this pin.

</Steps>

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Testing Motion">

Drag the handle and compare what you see with the calculated angles.

<Steps>

1. **Open position:** drag the handle to about 45° up. The handle and the main link are about 34° apart, and the clamp arm points about 12° above horizontal.

2. **Closed position:** drag the handle down to about -49°. At about -44° the handle and the main link lie in one line (dead center) and the clamp arm reaches the end of its travel, about -112°. At -49° the clamp arm has moved back by only about 0.5°.

</Steps>

A CAD assembly has no forces, so you cannot feel the lock. You can see its sign: near -44° the clamp arm almost stops while the handle still moves.

**Check it:** in FreeCAD 1.1 this model gave a clamp arm angle of 12.3° with the handle at 45°, -79.2° at 0°, -111.7° at -40°, -112.0° at -44° and -111.5° at -49°, the same as the cosine rule, with no two parts touching. Read the arm angle with **Tools → Measure** on the arm's top edge and a base edge (mode Angle). Right after the four pins the solver may give the arm at 112° (the other way of joining the bars); drag it to about 12° above horizontal, as the tip below says.

:::tip[If the handle will not reach -49°]
- Check the two typed distances: 40 mm between the base holes and 30 mm from Q to hole P
- If the handle and the main link never come into one line near -44°, the linkage has taken its second position, because these four bars can be joined in two ways. Open the handle to 45°, drag the clamp arm until it points about 12° above horizontal, and close the handle again
:::

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Part 11: Testing Parametric Control

<InArticleAd />


### Change Handle Length

A longer handle seems an easy way to get more force. In this model HandleLength is the distance from O to hole C, so a change moves hole C and changes the linkage. Try 130 mm.

<Steps>

1. Open the Spreadsheet

2. Change HandleLength from 120 to 130

3. Recompute (Ctrl+R)

4. Close the handle in the assembly. Dead center has moved from -44° to about -37°, because hole P must now be $130 - 80 = 50$ mm from O. Change HandleAngleClosed to -42 to keep 5° of over-center.

</Steps>

:::caution[Do not go to 150 mm]
At 150 mm, hole C in the open position is 125 mm from Q. The main link and the clamp arm together reach only $80 + 30 = 110$ mm, so the clamp cannot be assembled. For more leverage, make the grip longer beyond hole C and leave C where it is.
:::

### Change Over-Center Angle

<Steps>

1. Try OverCenterAngle = 2° (HandleAngleClosed = -46). The handle is close to dead center, so a knock or vibration can carry it back through and the clamp opens.

2. Try 10° (HandleAngleClosed = -54). The lock is safer, but the clamp arm moves back about 2° from the work, so the clamping force falls.

</Steps>

### Verify Motion Limits

A real clamp needs two stops for the handle, at 45° and at -49°. This model has none, so the handle can be dragged past both angles. Without the stop at -49° the work pushes the handle further and further past dead center, and the clamp arm lifts off.

## Design Verification

## Calculating Mechanical Advantage

<InArticleAd />


Mechanical advantage (MA) is the output force divided by the input force. In a toggle clamp it changes with the position of the handle. For links that turn, compare torques first. A torque is a force multiplied by its distance from the pivot, in N·mm.

The ratio of the torque on the clamp arm, $T_{\text{clamp arm}}$, to the torque that the hand puts on the handle, $T_{\text{handle}}$, depends on two angles:

$$
\frac{T_{\text{clamp arm}}}{T_{\text{handle}}} = \frac{c \sin\mu}{a \sin\nu}
$$

- $a$ is the handle length, O to C (120 mm)
- $c$ is the distance from the clamp arm pivot Q to hole P (30 mm)
- $\mu$ is the angle between the main link and the clamp arm, in degrees
- $\nu$ is the angle between the handle and the main link, in degrees

At dead center the handle and the main link are in line, so $\nu = 0^\circ$, $\sin\nu = 0$ and the ratio grows without limit. In a real clamp, friction and the small stretch of the parts set the limit. The angles below were found by solving the linkage at each handle angle.

**Open, handle at 45°:** $\nu = 33.8^\circ$ and $\mu = 113.5^\circ$.

$$
\frac{30 \times \sin 113.5^\circ}{120 \times \sin 33.8^\circ} = \frac{27.5}{66.8} = 0.41
$$

**Almost closed, handle at -40°:** $\nu = 1.99^\circ$ and $\mu = 106.3^\circ$.

$$
\frac{30 \times \sin 106.3^\circ}{120 \times \sin 1.99^\circ} = \frac{28.8}{4.16} = 6.9
$$

So in the last few degrees the same push on the handle gives about 17 times the torque that it gives in the open position. To get the ratio of forces, divide each torque by the distance to its force. With the hand at 120 mm from O and the pad at 100 mm from Q, the force ratio at -40° is $6.9 \times 120 / 100 = 8.3$.

## Common Issues and Solutions

<InArticleAd />


### The clamp does not lock

- **Cause:** the closed angle is before dead center, so the push from the work opens the handle
- **Fix:** calculate the dead center angle for your lengths, and set HandleAngleClosed a few degrees past it

### The handle cannot reach the closed angle

- **Cause:** the link lengths do not allow that position. At some handle angle hole C is further from Q than the main link and the clamp arm can reach
- **Fix:** check that the distance from C to Q is between $80 - 30 = 50$ mm and $80 + 30 = 110$ mm over the whole travel of the handle

### The handle passes through the clamp arm

- **Cause:** the handle and the clamp arm lie in one layer
- **Fix:** at pin P, pick the main link's front face and the clamp arm's back face, so the clamp arm stands on top of the main link; make pin Q Cylindrical
