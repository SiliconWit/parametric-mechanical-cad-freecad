A wiper linkage with one link 20 mm too long does not wipe. The motor turns part of the way, the links pull into one straight line, and the motor stalls or a pin breaks. Four lengths decide whether the driven link can turn a full circle, and one addition checks them before any metal is cut. In this lesson you model a four-bar linkage in FreeCAD from one master sketch that holds the four joint positions, so that every part follows when a length changes, and you test the rule on your own model. #FreeCAD #FourBarLinkage #GrashofTheorem #MasterSketch

## Engineering Context: Why This Mechanism Matters

<InArticleAd />


A four-bar linkage is four rigid links joined by four pin joints into a closed loop, with one link fixed. A motor turns one link, and another link swings or turns in answer. Wiper linkages, excavator arms, folding hinges and vehicle suspensions use it.

<YouTube videoId="F2NXWNiB2B4" title="Four-Bar Linkage (Crank-Rocker) Design in FreeCAD" />

### The Engineering Problem

A motor turns without stopping. An output link must swing back and forth through about 70°, as a wiper blade does. Which four lengths give this motion, and how do you make sure the motor is never stopped by the links?

## Mechanism Fundamentals

<InArticleAd />


### Components and Motion

The four links and the four joints, A to D, have these names in the lesson:

| Link | Name | Joins | What it does |
|------|------|-------|--------------|
| Link 1 | Ground | A to B | Fixed. Carries the two fixed pivots |
| Link 2 | Crank | A to C | The link the motor drives |
| Link 3 | Coupler | C to D | Joins the crank to the follower |
| Link 4 | Follower | B to D | The output link |

### Grashof's Theorem

Not every set of four lengths lets the crank turn fully. If the crank and the ground together are too long for the other two links to follow, the coupler and the follower stretch into one straight line and the crank stops. Grashof's theorem tells you from the lengths alone. Call the shortest length $S$, the longest $L$ and the other two $P$ and $Q$, all in mm:

$$S + L \leq P + Q$$

If this holds, the shortest link can turn fully against its neighbors, and its place in the loop decides the motion:

| The shortest link is | Motion | Name |
|----------------------|--------|------|
| The crank (link 2) | The crank turns fully, the follower swings | Crank-rocker |
| The ground (link 1) | The crank and the follower both turn fully | Double-crank |
| The coupler (link 3) | The crank and the follower both swing | Double-rocker |

If it does not hold, no link can turn fully.

### Dimensions for this lesson

The motion asks for a crank-rocker, so the crank must be the shortest link and the sum test must pass. The lesson uses 100, 40, 80 and 70 mm for links 1 to 4:

1. Shortest and longest: $S + L = 40 + 100 = 140 \text{ mm}$ ✅
2. The other two: $P + Q = 80 + 70 = 150 \text{ mm}$ ✅
3. Test: $140 \leq 150$, and the shortest link is the crank, so this is a crank-rocker ✅

With these lengths the follower swings through 69.8°. The margin is only 10 mm: a crank of 50 mm is exactly at the limit, and a crank of 60 mm fails.

## What You'll Build

<InArticleAd />


One master sketch, four link plates that take their joint positions from it, an assembly that moves, and a drawing of one plate.

## Parametric Design Strategy

<InArticleAd />


In a model where each part has its own sketch, two parts agree only if you type the same numbers in both. A four-bar linkage has four joints, and each joint is shared by two parts. If one hole is 1 mm away from its partner, the loop does not close.

A **master sketch** holds the four joint positions once, as a stick drawing of the linkage. It makes no solid. Each part copies its two joints from it, and the lengths in the master sketch come from the spreadsheet:

```text
 ┌─────────────┐    ┌───────────────┐    ┌──────────────┐
 │ Spreadsheet │    │ Master sketch │    │ Four parts   │
 │ 4 lengths   ├───►│ joints        ├───►│ two joints   │
 │             │    │ A, B, C, D    │    │ each         │
 └─────────────┘    └───────────────┘    └──────────────┘
```

So the order of work is: spreadsheet, master sketch, four links, assembly, drawing, and then tests of the theorem on the model.

## Part 4: Creating the Master Sketch

<InArticleAd />


### What is a Master Sketch?

The master sketch is four lines, one for each link, joined end to end. Three kinds of constraint make it exact, and you met them in the slider crank:

- **Coincident** joins the end of one line to the end of the next. This is the pin joint.
- **Distance** gives each line its length from the spreadsheet.
- **Horizontal** and a coincident point on the origin hold the ground link in place.

A sketch counts the ways in which it can still move as **degrees of freedom**. A normal part sketch must reach zero. This one is different: after the constraints above it keeps exactly one, and that one is the crank angle, the motion of the real linkage.

### Creating the Master Sketch

<Steps>

1. **Ensure you're in Part Design workbench**

2. **Create new sketch on XY_Plane**

   - Click **Create Sketch** button
   - Select **XY_Plane**
   - Click **OK**

   This sketch does not belong to a body. It only carries the joint positions.

</Steps>

### Drawing the Linkage Layout

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Ground Link (Link 1)">

**Draw a horizontal line:**

<Steps>

1. **Line tool** (press G, L)

2. Click at **origin** (0, 0)

3. Move horizontally to the right

4. Click to place endpoint

5. Press **Escape**

6. **Make it horizontal:**
   - Select the line
   - Press **H** key

7. **Fix the start point:**
   - Coincident constraint
   - Click left endpoint
   - Click origin

8. **Dimension the link:**
   - Distance constraint
   - Click both endpoints
   - Click **ƒx** button
   - Formula: `Spreadsheet.Link1`
   - Press **Enter**

</Steps>

This is the **ground link**. Its left end, on the origin, is joint **A** and its right end is joint **B**.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Crank (Link 2)">

**Draw a line from point A:**

<Steps>

1. **Line tool** (G, L)

2. Start at the **left endpoint** of Link 1 (point A)

3. Draw at an angle upward-right

4. Click to place endpoint

5. Press **Escape**

6. **Constrain the link:**
   - Coincident constraint
   - Click start of new line
   - Click left endpoint of Link 1 (point A)

7. **Dimension the length:**
   - Distance constraint
   - Click both endpoints of the new line
   - Click **ƒx** button
   - Formula: `Spreadsheet.Link2`
   - Press **Enter**

</Steps>

This is the **crank**, connecting joints **A** and **C**.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Follower (Link 4)">

**Draw a line from point B:**

<Steps>

1. **Line tool** (G, L)

2. Start at **right endpoint** of Link 1 (point B)

3. Draw upward at an angle

4. Click to place endpoint

5. Press **Escape**

6. **Constrain:**
   - Coincident at point B
   - Distance = `Spreadsheet.Link4`

</Steps>

This is the **follower**, connecting joints **B** and **D**.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Coupler (Link 3)">

**Draw a line connecting the free ends:**

<Steps>

1. **Line tool** (G, L)

2. Start at free end of Link 2 (point **C**)

3. End at free end of Link 4 (point **D**)

4. Press **Escape**

5. **Constrain:**
   - Coincident at point C
   - Coincident at point D
   - Distance = `Spreadsheet.Link3`

</Steps>

The four lines now form a closed loop.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Construction Geometry">

Construction geometry is a line that guides other geometry and makes no solid. The four lines are a stick drawing, not a part, so mark them as construction:

<Steps>

1. **Select all four lines**
   - Press **Ctrl+A** or box-select all lines

2. **Toggle construction geometry**
   - Press **G, N**
   - Lines turn blue/dashed

</Steps>

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Finalize">

<Steps>

1. **Check constraints:**

   The solver should report exactly 1 degree of freedom, the crank angle. The count agrees: 3 moving links with 3 freedoms each, less 2 for each of the 4 pin joints, gives $3(4-1) - 2(4) = 1$. Verify:
   - Link 1: horizontal, one end at origin, length ✓
   - Link 2: coincident at A, length ✓
   - Link 3: coincident at C and D, length ✓
   - Link 4: coincident at B, length ✓

2. **Add an angle dimension**

   The parts need one fixed position to copy, so remove the last degree of freedom with an angle on Link 2:
   - Angle constraint
   - Link 2 to horizontal
   - Type: `30` degrees

   To see the linkage in another position later, change this one number.

3. **Close the sketch**

   Click **Close** button in toolbar

</Steps>

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Part 5: Creating Link 1 (Ground Link)

<InArticleAd />


### Design Intent

Link 1 is a flat plate with a pin hole at joint A and another at joint B. Its sketch does not place the holes with dimensions of its own. It copies the two joint points from the master sketch with the **External geometry** tool, which brings geometry of another sketch into this one as a reference that follows the original.

### Creating Link 1

<Steps>

1. **Create a Body**

   - Click **Create Body** button
   - Right-click Body → **Rename**
   - Type: `Link1_Ground`

2. **Create a Sketch**

   - Select Link1_Ground body
   - Create sketch on **XY_Plane**

</Steps>

### Drawing the Link Plate

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="External References">

**Reference master sketch geometry:**

<Steps>

1. **External geometry tool** (press G, X)

2. Click the **master sketch** in the tree

3. Select **joint A endpoint** (origin)

4. Select **joint B endpoint** (right end of Link 1)

5. Press **Escape**

</Steps>

The selected points now appear in purple. They follow the master sketch, and you cannot move them here.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Pin Holes">

**Draw circles at both joints:**

<Steps>

1. **Circle tool** (G, C)

2. Draw circle centered at **origin** (joint A)

3. Press **Escape**

4. **Circle tool** again

5. Draw circle at **joint B** reference point

6. Press **Escape**

7. **Constrain both circles:**
   - Coincident: First circle center → origin reference
   - Coincident: Second circle center → joint B reference

8. **Dimension the holes:**
   - Radius constraint on first circle
   - Click **ƒx** → `Spreadsheet.PinRadius`
   - Repeat for second circle

</Steps>

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Link Body">

<Steps>

1. **Rectangle tool**

2. Draw rectangle connecting the two circles

3. Press **Escape**

4. **Symmetric constraint:**
   - Select top edge of rectangle
   - Select bottom edge of rectangle
   - Select horizontal centerline between holes
   - This centers the rectangle on the link axis

5. **Dimension width:**
   - Distance constraint (perpendicular to link)
   - Click **ƒx** → `Spreadsheet.LinkWidth`

6. **Lock the two ends:**
   - Distance from the center of each hole to the short edge beyond it
   - Click **ƒx** → `Spreadsheet.LinkWidth / 2`

   Without these two, the solver reports 2 degrees of freedom: each end of the plate can slide. With them the plate is Link1 + LinkWidth long, 120 mm.

</Steps>

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Finalize">

<Steps>

1. **Verify fully constrained**

   Check solver messages panel

2. **Close sketch**

   Click **Close** button

3. **Pad the link**

   - Select the sketch
   - Click **Pad** tool
   - Length: Click **ƒx** → `Spreadsheet.LinkThickness`
   - Click **OK**

</Steps>

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Part 6: Creating Link 2 (Crank)

<InArticleAd />


### Quick Creation Process

Links 2, 3 and 4 repeat the steps of Link 1 with other joint points.

:::caution[These links are not horizontal]
In the master sketch Link 2 stands at 30°, and Links 3 and 4 are also inclined. The Rectangle tool makes sides that are held horizontal and vertical, so its rectangle cannot follow an inclined link. Draw the outline with four lines, and make the long sides parallel to the line between the two joints.
:::

<Steps>

1. **Create Body → Rename to `Link2_Crank`**

2. **Create Sketch on XY_Plane**

3. **Reference Master Sketch Geometry:**
   - External geometry tool (G, X)
   - Select master sketch from tree
   - Click **Link 2 endpoints** (point A and point C)
   - Purple reference points appear

4. **Draw the Link:**
   - Circle at point A, radius = `Spreadsheet.PinRadius`
   - Circle at point C, radius = `Spreadsheet.PinRadius`
   - Rectangle connecting them
   - Symmetric constraint about Link 2 centerline
   - Width = `Spreadsheet.LinkWidth`
   - Each end `Spreadsheet.LinkWidth / 2` beyond its hole center

5. **Verify fully constrained and Close sketch**

6. **Pad:**
   - Thickness = `Spreadsheet.LinkThickness`

</Steps>

## Part 9: Assembly

<InArticleAd />


The master sketch shows the linkage in one position. An assembly joins the four plates with constraints between parts, so that you can drag the crank and watch the motion that the theorem predicts.

:::caution[Which Assembly workbench these steps use]
These steps were written for FreeCAD 0.21, which has no assembly workbench of its own: you install one from **Tools → Addon manager**. The names below (Create Assembly, Fixed, Axial Align) are nearest to the **Assembly3** add-on, which says Axial Alignment. A2plus calls the same constraint axisCoincident. FreeCAD 1.0 and later have a built-in Assembly workbench with other names: ground Link 1 where this lesson says Fixed, and use a **Revolute** joint where it says Axial Align.
:::

### Assembly Strategy

Link 1 is fixed. Each of the four joints is a pin, and a pin must leave rotation free. So at each joint you line up two hole axes with **Axial Align**, and you never fix one moving link to another.

### Creating the Assembly

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Setup">

<Steps>

1. **Switch to Assembly workbench**

   Use workbench dropdown

2. **Create new assembly**

   Assembly → Create Assembly

3. **Add all four parts**

   Drag from tree or use "Add Part" button:
   - Link1_Ground
   - Link2_Crank
   - Link3_Coupler
   - Link4_Follower

</Steps>

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Ground Link">

<Steps>

1. Select **Link1_Ground** in assembly tree

2. Click **Fixed constraint** button

3. Link 1 is now locked in place. All motion is measured from it

</Steps>

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Pin Joints">

**Apply Axial Align constraints at all four joints:**

<Steps>

1. **Joint A (Link 1 - Link 2):**
   - Select hole axis from Link 1 at point A
   - Select hole axis from Link 2 at point A
   - Click **Axial Align** constraint

2. **Joint B (Link 1 - Link 4):**
   - Link 1 hole at B
   - Link 4 hole at B
   - Axial Align

3. **Joint C (Link 2 - Link 3):**
   - Link 2 hole at C
   - Link 3 hole at C
   - Axial Align

4. **Joint D (Link 3 - Link 4):**
   - Link 3 hole at D
   - Link 4 hole at D
   - Axial Align

</Steps>

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Testing">

**Test the mechanism:**

<Steps>

1. **Drag Link 2** (the crank) in the assembly view

2. **Compare with the prediction:**
   - Link 2 turns through a full 360°
   - Link 4 swings through about 70° and returns
   - Link 4 never makes a full turn

</Steps>

This is the crank-rocker that the sum test predicted from the lengths alone.

:::caution[If Mechanism Won't Move]
- Check that **only Link 1** has Fixed constraint
- All pin joints should be **Axial Align** (not Fixed!)
- Remove any redundant constraints
- Ensure proper hole alignment
:::

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Part 11: Testing Parametric Control

<InArticleAd />


Now change lengths and see what the model does. Before each recompute, add the shortest to the longest and compare with the other two, so that you know what to expect.

### Verification Tests

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Test 1: Basic Update">

<Steps>

1. **Open Spreadsheet**

   Double-click Spreadsheet in tree

2. **Change Link2 to 50mm**

   - Click cell B3
   - Type: `50`
   - Press Enter

3. **Recompute**

   Press **Ctrl+R** or click Recompute button

4. **Observe changes:**

   - The crank line in the master sketch is longer
   - Joint C has moved, and with it the holes of Link 2 and Link 3
   - The assembly still closes

</Steps>

One number moved a joint in three objects. Note that 50 mm is exactly the limit, $50 + 100 = 80 + 70$. Set Link2 back to 40 before you test motion.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Test 2: Grashof Configurations">

**Configuration 1: Crank-Rocker (original)**

Link1 = 100, Link2 = 40, Link3 = 80, Link4 = 70

$$S + L = 40 + 100 = 140 \leq P + Q = 80 + 70 = 150 \text{ (Grashof ✓)}$$

- Link2 rotates fully 360°
- Link4 oscillates

**Configuration 2: Double-Rocker**

Link1 = 100, Link2 = 80, Link3 = 40, Link4 = 70

$$S + L = 40 + 100 = 140 \leq P + Q = 80 + 70 = 150 \text{ (Grashof ✓)}$$

- Now Link3 (the coupler) is shortest
- Both links joined to ground oscillate, and only the coupler can turn fully
- If instead the shortest link is the ground (Link1 = 40, Link2 = 100), both Link2 and Link4 rotate fully: a double-crank

**Configuration 3: Non-Grashof**

Link1 = 100, Link2 = 60, Link3 = 80, Link4 = 70

$$S + L = 60 + 100 = 160 > P + Q = 80 + 70 = 150 \text{ (not Grashof)}$$

- No link can rotate fully: Link2 only rocks between two limits
- Compare with Link2 = 50, where $S + L = 150 = P + Q$: the limiting case, in which the links can line up and the mechanism may lock up

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Test 3: Extreme Values">

<Steps>

1. Set **Link2 = 60, Link3 = 120**

2. Recompute

3. The model rebuilds, but check the sums: $60 + 120 = 180$ is more than $100 + 70 = 170$, so this linkage cannot turn fully

</Steps>

Try different ratios:
- Long coupler: Link3 = 150
- Short follower: Link4 = 40
- Balanced: All links ≈ 80mm

:::caution[When the sketch cannot be solved]
With Link3 = 150 and the crank at 30°, the loop cannot close. The end of the crank is less than 70 mm from joint B, and links of 150 mm and 70 mm cannot span less than $150 - 70 = 80 \text{ mm}$. The master sketch reports a solver error. The error is correct: the linkage does not exist in that position. Set the crank angle to 60° and it closes.
:::

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

:::tip[Instrument it]
The CAD model predicts how the output rocker swings for a given crank angle; instrument the built linkage to check it. Put a rotary encoder (a magnetic AS5600) on the input crank and another on the output joint, and the measured angle relationship is the real coupler motion, complete with the play your model leaves out. An [ESP32 or STM32](/education/sensor-actuator-interfacing-stm32/) reads both encoders, and the live input-versus-output trace can [sit beside the predicted curve on siliconwit.io](https://siliconwit.io) so wear and a loose joint show up as the two drifting apart.
:::

## Common Issues and Solutions

<InArticleAd />


| Problem | What to check |
|---------|---------------|
| Parts do not line up in the assembly | Every part sketch must take its two points from the same master sketch, with the External Geometry tool (G, X) |
| The assembly does not move | Only Link 1 is Fixed. Every joint is Axial Align, and no moving link is fixed to another |
| The master sketch has more than 1 degree of freedom | Each line needs its length, and the left end of Link 1 must be on the origin |
| A part does not follow the spreadsheet | The dimension must show the ƒx sign. Check that the alias exists |
| The master sketch gives a solver error after a change | The four lengths cannot close at this crank angle. Check the sums, or change the angle |
